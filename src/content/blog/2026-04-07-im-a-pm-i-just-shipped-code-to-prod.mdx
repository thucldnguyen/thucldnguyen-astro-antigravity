## Synopsis

**Three days. Two PRs. Production.**

Leadership announced on **March 26** that every PM should ship **2 PRs by June 30**. I merged both of mine on **March 29**.

This post is the takeaway: code generation is the easy part now. Judgment, testing rigor, and choosing the right problem are the hard parts.

---

Merging those 2 PRs felt great.

Not because the code was especially hard. It wasn’t.

What mattered was getting real fixes through a real production process, at a company where the bar is high and the blast radius is global.

---

## The Bugs Were Small. The Friction Wasn’t.

Both PRs fixed annoying UI issues in our media player.

- One fixed a bug that had been overlooked by a sister team.
- The other removed persistent tooltips that kept getting in the way when users tried to edit clips or markers in evidence videos.

Users feel that kind of friction immediately.

---

## AI Made Coding Easy. It Didn’t Make Shipping Easy.

I used Claude Opus 4.6 with 1M context. The long context window made it easy to stay in flow, understand the codebase, and get to a working fix quickly.

But writing the code was the easy part. Testing was the hard part.

At Axon, PR review bots are strict for a reason. We ship to production environments across the US, UK, Canada, Latin America, Australia, and more. When the stakes are that high, “it works on my machine” means nothing.

Shipping is not just generating code. Shipping is surviving rigor.

After this sprint, I checked my Claude usage to make sure I was using AI aggressively *and* responsibly.

Most PMs won’t spend as much as engineers, and that’s fine. But if you’re spending $2 out of a $1,000 monthly cap, you probably don’t have a cost problem — you have an adoption problem.

At Axon, my April Claude usage is about $696 out of $1,000, still within budget:

![Anthropic usage dashboard showing 70% of monthly budget used](../../assets/anthropic-usage-70-percent.jpg)

Don’t overspend. But don’t underuse the leverage either.

---

## PMs Should Pass the Fitness Test (Without Becoming Discount Engineers)

PMs should be able to ship a real production change when needed.

That skill is increasingly valuable in startups and on capacity-constrained teams. If you can fix a paper cut yourself, unblock a small issue, or validate an idea without waiting in line, that’s real leverage.

But there’s a boundary: if a PM is shipping code all day, every day, that role should probably be an engineer. PMs should be comfortable coding, not pretending to be full-time engineers.

---

## The Human Edge Still Wins

I still jump on late-night customer calls 3–4 times a week.

Those conversations are where the real product signal comes from: where users hesitate, what they ignore, and what slows them down under pressure. That context is what tells you *which* bug matters now versus later.

AI can write code. It still can’t build trust with users or extract messy human context the way direct contact can.

---

## Speed Is Cheap. Judgment Is Not.

AI makes it easier to build fast. That makes problem selection more important, not less.

Solving the wrong problem quickly is still failure.

This is also why PM-to-engineering ratios are getting compressed: engineering output is being amplified faster than PM/design output. Lenny captured this well in his summary of Anthropic’s Head of Growth, Amol Avasare ([post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/lennyrachitsky_my-biggest-takeaways-from-anthropics-head-activity-7446932397385818112-QFR8)).

My take: PMs are not becoming less important. We’re becoming more of the bottleneck.

The new bar is clear: be technical enough to earn leverage, and disciplined enough to choose the right problem.
