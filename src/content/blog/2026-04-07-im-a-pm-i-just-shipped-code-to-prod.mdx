**I merged two PRs to production last week. Both were approved by senior engineers. Both shipped three days after leadership announced a new PM goal: ship two PRs by June.**

I finished mine about 2.5 months early.

It felt great — not because the code was hard. It wasn’t. It felt great because these were real fixes that survived a real production process at a company with a high bar and global blast radius.

---

## The Bugs Were Small. The Friction Wasn’t.

Both PRs fixed annoying UI issues in our media player.

- One fixed a bug that had been overlooked by a sister team.
- One removed persistent tooltips that blocked users when editing clips or markers in evidence videos.

Not glamorous. Still important.

Users don’t care whether a bug is “small.” They care whether the product gets in their way.

---

## AI Made Coding Easy. It Didn’t Make Shipping Easy.

I used Claude Opus 4.6 with 1M context. It was excellent for staying in flow, understanding the codebase, and getting to a working fix quickly.

But writing code was the easy part. Testing was the hard part.

At Axon, review bots are strict for a reason. We ship to production environments across the US, UK, Canada, Latin America, Australia, and more. When the stakes are that high, “it works on my machine” is noise.

Shipping isn’t code generation. Shipping is surviving rigor.

---

## PMs Should Pass the Fitness Test

PMs should be able to ship a real production change when needed.

That skill matters even more in startups and capacity-constrained teams. If you can fix a paper cut yourself, unblock a small issue, or validate an idea without waiting in line, that’s real leverage.

PMs should pass that fitness test.

---

## But Don’t Become a Discount Engineer

There’s a boundary.

If a PM ships code all day, every day, that role should probably be an engineer.

PMs should be comfortable coding. PMs should not spend their highest-value hours pretending to be full-time engineers.

That is not where the leverage is.

---

## The Human Edge Still Wins

A PM’s core edge is still human:

- talking to customers,
- understanding pain deeply,
- noticing patterns,
- choosing the right problems.

That’s why I still jump on late-night customer calls 3–4 times a week.

AI can write code. It still can’t build trust with users or interpret messy human context the way direct customer contact can.

---

## The PM-to-Engineering Ratio Is Breaking

Amol from Anthropic recently made a point that stuck with me: the classic 1 PM to 6 engineers model is breaking.

Engineering is getting the biggest AI leverage boost. A five-engineer team can now output far more than before. PM and design leverage haven’t scaled at the same rate.

So one PM is increasingly responsible for a wider execution surface.

My take: PMs are not becoming less important. PMs are becoming the bottleneck.

---

## Speed Is Cheap. Judgment Is Not.

AI makes it easier to build fast.

That makes problem selection more important, not less.

Solving the wrong problem quickly is still failure — just faster failure.

The new bar for PMs is clear: be technical enough to earn leverage, but disciplined enough to stay focused on the highest-value work.

Choosing the right problem, fast, has never mattered more.

---

If you’re a PM, learn enough technical depth to ship a real fix. Then spend the rest of your time where machines still struggle: customer truth, product judgment, and ruthless prioritization.
